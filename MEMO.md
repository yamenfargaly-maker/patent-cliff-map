# The 2030 Patent Cliff Map

**Which large-cap pharma companies have to buy growth before 2032, which ones can afford to, and what is left to buy**

*17 August 2026 · Independent research · Not investment advice*

---

## Thesis in one paragraph

Nineteen large-cap pharmaceutical companies lose **$293.2B of annual revenue to patent expiry between 2027 and 2032 — 32.3% of their combined FY2025 revenue of $908B**, net of $15.3B of franchises booked twice across partner pairs. Against that they hold roughly **$520B of debt capacity** at a 3.0× leverage ceiling: 1.77× coverage in aggregate. The aggregate is misleading. Amgen, GSK and Pfizer carry **16% of the industry's exposure against 8% of its firepower**, while Lilly, J&J and Sanofi hold **31% of the firepower against 21% of the exposure**. That mismatch, not the raw size of the cliff, is what should drive deal flow through 2028. It predicts three things: stock-funded consolidation among the constrained (the reported AstraZeneca–Bristol Myers approach in August 2026 is the first visible instance), divestiture rather than acquisition from Takeda and Bayer, and continued price discipline from the cash-rich buyers who do not actually need to transact.

**This document has been through a full adversarial audit.** Every figure was independently re-verified against primary filings, and the audit changed the conclusions — it moved Merck out of the top urgency slot, removed Bayer from the cliff narrative entirely, cut every precedent premium by 6–10 points, and took AbbVie's post-Apogee firepower to zero. Section 8 lists what changed and why. The pre-audit version of this memo was wrong in ways that would have been visible to anyone who checked.

## 1. The cliff is concentrated in four molecules

$106.5B — **36% of the entire 2027–32 exposure** — sits in four franchises:

| Molecule | Owner | FY2025 revenue | US LOE | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Semaglutide | Novo Nordisk | $34.6B | 2032 | Novo's 20-F guides EU/Japan to **2034** with SPCs, so only the US leg lands inside the window. Canada is **already generic** — Health Canada approved Dr. Reddy's on 28 Apr 2026 after Novo let the compound patent lapse for an unpaid maintenance fee |
| Keytruda | Merck | $31.6B | 2028 | 48.7% of Merck's revenue. Two patent-term-adjustment patents run to 2029 and Merck's 10-K says it expects litigation |
| Eliquis | BMS $14.4B + Pfizer $8.0B | $22.4B | 1 Apr 2028 | Settlement date for the settling filers; three non-settling generics are blocked to the 2031 formulation patent. Pfizer's 10-K flags a downside as early as Nov 2026 if the composition patent falls |
| Dupixent | Sanofi $17.8B + Regeneron $5.2B\* | $23.0B | Mar 2031 | Composition patent expires Oct 2027; PTE carries it to Mar 2031; Sanofi asserts formulation patents to 2045 |

The single most important non-consensus item in the dataset runs the other way. **Gilead's Biktarvy — 49% of its revenue — moved from a 2033 LOE to 1 April 2036** via settlements with Lupin, Cipla and Laurus. Any model built on pre-2025 assumptions materially understates Gilead. **AbbVie's Rinvoq similarly settled to 2037.** Together that is $22.6B of revenue reclassified out of the danger window, and it is why both names screen far calmer here than they did eighteen months ago.

## 3. Firepower, and why the aggregate lies

Capacity is modelled as `max(0, 3.0 × LTM EBITDA − pro-forma net debt)`, using 30 June 2026 balance sheets adjusted for announced-but-unclosed deals (AbbVie/Apogee $10.9B, GSK/Nuvalent $10.6B, Vertex/Crinetics $8.8B).

**Coverage ratio — firepower per dollar of revenue at risk:**

| Cannot self-fund | Coverage | | Comfortable | Coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bayer | **0.00×** | | Vertex | 33.6× |
| AbbVie | **0.00×** | | Regeneron | 4.95× |
| Takeda | **0.16×** | | Eli Lilly | 3.51× |
| GSK | **0.60×** | | Roche | 2.77× |
| Amgen | **0.61×** | | Johnson & Johnson | 2.75× |
| Bristol Myers | **0.91×** | | Biogen | 2.18× |

**AbbVie's zero is a finding, not an artefact.** Correcting its LTM EBITDA from an aggregator's $30.76B to $24.74B — computed from AbbVie's own reported operating income plus D&A — and adding the $10.9B Apogee deal takes pro-forma net debt to ~$75.2B against a 3.0× ceiling of $74.2B. AbbVie is *through* its own leverage ceiling on this basis, which is consistent with CFO Scott Reents's stated commitment to get back to 2.0× within two to three years of closing. It is not buying anything else soon.

**Bristol Myers at 0.91× is the sharper problem.** Second-deepest cliff in the industry, landing 2028, and it can raise roughly $25B against a $27B hole. It has not done a large acquisition since Karuna in 2023 and has spent 2026 paying down debt. Either it transacts within four quarters or it is transacted upon.

Two places where the model deliberately disagrees with management, and management is probably right:

- **Pfizer** computes to $24.6B of capacity. Albert Bourla guided on the Q2-2026 call (4 Aug) to **$6–7B of remaining BD capacity**, against >$80B deployed since 2022. The model measures what the balance sheet permits; Pfizer is managing to a rating and a dividend it has said it will defend.
- **Gilead** computes to $20.7B and screens as sidelined — but management said outright it does not anticipate additional sizeable M&A this year, after committing ~$15B in five months to Arcellx, Tubulis and Ouro.

Read the model's firepower number as *theoretical capacity* and the management quote as *stated intent*. The gap between them is the interesting part.

**On the EBITDA definition, because it is load-bearing.** EBITDA here is stated *before acquired IPR&D and non-recurring items* — the basis a rating agency applies to a leverage test. On strict GAAP the 2026 figures are unusable for this purpose: Merck expensed $14.7B of acquired IPR&D in H1-2026 (Cidara, Terns), which takes its GAAP LTM EBITDA to roughly $12B and its leverage to ~3.9×; Gilead expensed $11.3B (Arcellx, Tubulis, Ouro) plus a $1.75B impairment and posted an H1 operating loss, making its GAAP leverage undefined. Those are real cash outflows, but they are already captured in net debt — running them through EBITDA as well would charge the same dollar twice. Anyone rebuilding this from a screener will get very different leverage ratios, and the difference is definitional rather than factual.

## 4. The four quadrants — the actual call

**Must buy · can buy** (BMS, Novo, Merck, Novartis, Roche, AstraZeneca, Regeneron). Seven names, 54% of the exposure, 52% of the firepower. This is where the deal flow comes from. **Roche is the most under-deployed name in the industry**: 0.92× levered, AA-rated, $63.9B of capacity, nothing transformative done, and a $23.1B cliff led by Ocrevus. Bristol Myers sits in this quadrant only marginally — its 0.91× coverage is the worst of the seven.

**Must buy · can't buy** (Amgen, GSK, Pfizer). 16% of exposure, 8% of firepower. Amgen is 2.5× levered on a negative outlook with seven LOEs clustered in 2027–30. GSK has spent its capacity on Nuvalent ahead of the dolutegravir cliff. Pfizer computes to more room than it says it has. Their options are stock, divestiture, or being acquired — which is the frame for the **reported AstraZeneca–Bristol Myers ~$400B approach in early August 2026**, reported and then denied by a senior source to Reuters on 5 August, with no merger filing in either company's EDGAR record. Treat it as a signal about the constraint, not a live deal.

**Can buy · needn't** (Lilly, J&J, Sanofi). 31% of the firepower against 21% of the exposure. These are the price-setters. J&J's management says acquisitions "are not a requirement to deliver on our objectives"; Len Schleifer says Regeneron is "not allergic to any external opportunity… but we are pretty disciplined." Expect them to buy at their price and walk when they don't get it. Lilly is the anomaly — the most prolific acquirer in the market with 12+ transactions in 2026, and it is in the quadrant that needs to do none of them.

**Sidelined** (Gilead, Biogen, Bayer, Takeda, AbbVie, Vertex). Either the cliff already happened (Biogen, Bayer), moved out by settlement (Gilead to 2036, AbbVie's Rinvoq to 2037), never existed (Vertex, protected to 2037), or the balance sheet forecloses it (Takeda at 0.16× coverage, AbbVie at zero).

**Summit ranks first** because it is the literal Keytruda-successor modality — a PD-1×VEGF bispecific — and its two natural buyers are the two most urgent companies in the industry, both of whom can fund it. The complication is real and should be in any pitch: Summit carries a **reiterated going-concern warning** at a $10.7B market cap, and the stock is down 47% year-on-year. That is either the reason it gets bought or the reason it doesn't.

**Revolution Medicines is the instructive failure.** It scores 100 on materiality — strategically the best fit in the universe for Merck — and **zero on fundability**. At a $45.3B market cap, even a size-tapered premium implies a takeout above every single acquirer's modelled capacity. Merck did pursue it: the offer was **$28–32B and talks collapsed over price on 26 January 2026**. No deal was signed and Revolution has since done an independent partnership with BeOne Medicines. The model reproduces the reason without being told about it, which is the most useful validation in this exercise. Note the date matters — $28–32B was struck below today's $45.3B market cap, so quoting it undated makes it look like a discount bid.

**Kymera is the highest-conviction defensive name.** An oral STAT6 degrader is an oral Dupixent, and Dupixent is 36% of Sanofi's revenue with an LOE date that could be 2027 or 2031 depending on how the PTE and formulation patents hold. Sanofi and Regeneron both have the capacity. It scores 75.0 only because a Ph2 asset scores lower on materiality — that is a modelling artefact of using takeout size as the proxy for strategic weight, and it is the clearest place where judgement should override the score.

## 6. Method

- **Window.** Products whose primary US exclusivity falls 2027–2032 inclusive. Assets already eroding (Stelara, Prolia, Entresto, Xarelto, Eylea, Revlimid, Perjeta) are excluded — including them would double-count declines already in the run-rate.
- **Urgency** = 0.70 × min(% at risk / 75%, 1) × 100 + 0.30 × proximity of the peak expiry year within the window.
- **Firepower** = max(0, 3.0 × LTM EBITDA − pro-forma net debt), EBITDA before acquired IPR&D. 3.0× is the leverage an A−/BBB+ issuer can defend; it is a modelling convention, not a company target.
- **Aggregate exposure is de-duplicated.** Three franchises are booked by two companies each: Pfizer's Eliquis alliance revenue ($7.96B) derives from BMS's gross Eliquis sales, Merck's Lynparza alliance revenue ($1.45B) from AstraZeneca's, and Regeneron's collaboration revenue ($5.9B) from Sanofi's Dupixent. Per-company lines are correct as stated; the portfolio total subtracts $15.3B.
- **Premiums** are medians from a hand-built sample of pharma/biotech acquisitions ≥$500M announced Jan-2024 to Aug-2026, **rebuilt on a single basis** — premium to prior-day close, or the unaffected close where a leak is identifiable.

| Phase at announcement | n | Median | IQR | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preclinical / Ph1 | 7 | 105.0% | 41–118% | **Directional only** |
| Phase 2 | 10 | 54.0% | — | Caveat |
| Phase 3 / filed | 10 | 43.5% | — | Usable |
| Commercial | 14 | 36.5% | — | Usable |

A size taper is applied above $5B market cap, floored at 0.50×, reflecting the observed compression from a 76.8% median in 2024 to ~40% in 2026 as median deal size tripled. That year trend is the most robust statistic here and is corroborated by [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/m-and-a/our-insights/life-sciences-dealmaking-gains-momentum-as-strategic-pressures-intensify), which reports premiums fell nearly 40% in 2025 from 2020–24 levels.

**There is no published benchmark to check this against.** No investment bank or data provider — Stifel, Leerink, Evaluate, DealForma, PitchBook, LSEG — publishes a median takeover premium stratified by clinical phase for this period. This analysis *is* the estimate; it is not validated against an external standard, and it should not be presented as though it were.

## 7. What this model gets wrong

**Erosion is not modelled.** Raw at-risk revenue overstates the economic hole. Real 2024–26 curves: multi-source oral generics lose 55–70% in year one and 80–90% by year two; self-administered biologics facing PBM re-contracting lose ~40% in year one (Stelara fell from $10.36B to $6.08B, −41.3%, in twelve months — the old "slow biosimilar erosion" heuristic is obsolete); IV oncology biologics lose 40–60% by year two *unless* the sponsor converts to subcutaneous first; and Prolia is the counter-example, losing US exclusivity in February 2025 with 2025 sales flat.

**Vaccines are now excluded, and that changed the answer.** The first version of this model treated Merck's Gardasil ($5.2B) and Vaxneuvance ($0.8B) and GSK's Shingrix ($3.6B) as at-risk because they appear in patent tables. They should not be. FDA has never approved a vaccine under the 351(k) biosimilar pathway — a competitor needs a full BLA with clinical endpoints, which is a competitive-entry event on its own timetable, not an LOE event. The empirical proof is in Merck's own disclosure: **Gardasil patents have already expired in the EU, Japan and China and no biosimilar has entered any of them.** Removing vaccines cut Merck's exposure from $40.2B to $34.1B and moved Bristol Myers to the top of the urgency ranking. (Gardasil does face real competition from Chinese domestic entrants via full BLAs — that belongs in a separate competitive-entry line with different timing.)

**Capacity is treated as spendable.** It is not, for the reasons in §3.

**Premiums have thin support.** The preclinical/Ph1 bucket spans 6% (Terns) to 215% (Poseida) across seven observations, and every single one moves the median. Do not use it as a point estimate. Basis inconsistency is the larger issue: across twelve deals disclosing both, the median gap between a prior-close and a VWAP premium is **31 points**. Akero alone discloses four different premiums for one transaction. The first version of this memo mixed bases and consequently overstated every clinical bucket by 6–10 points.

**Data quality is uneven and now flagged per company.** Roche and Novartis publish no product-level patent expiry table. Roche discloses nothing for Ocrevus, Hemlibra, Tecentriq, Kadcyla or Alecensa; Novartis's only company statement is that Cosentyx expires "around the end of the decade." Roughly $43B of at-risk revenue rests on dates that should be read as **unknown**, not estimated. Takeda's revenue figure is unverified and its fiscal year ends 31 March, so it is not period-comparable to the rest. Twelve LOE dates are disputed or in litigation and belong in scenarios rather than point estimates — the largest being Dupixent (Oct 2027 vs. Mar 2031 vs. 2045) and Keytruda (2028 vs. 2029).

**The target universe decays fast.** 2026 has been the heaviest M&A year on record — ~$189B of disclosed value through mid-August. Five of GEN's ten "top takeover targets of 2026" were acquired inside seven months. Two names on common lists — Arcellx (Gilead, closed April) and Apellis (Biogen, closed May) — still return live-looking quotes on aggregator pages; a bulk price pull without a delisting filter will silently screen in dead companies.

## 9. The probability-of-success layer

The target valuations above are precedent multiples — what acquirers have paid for comparable assets. That answers "what would this trade at," not "what is it worth." This section replaces the multiple with a risk-adjusted NPV built from the asset's own probability of reaching approval.

**Priors.** Phase transitions come from BIO / Informa Pharma Intelligence / QLS Advisors, *Clinical Development Success Rates and Contributing Factors 2011–2020* — 12,728 transitions, 9,704 programs, 1,779 companies. It is preferred to Wong, Siah & Lo (2019) because it is the most recent complete phase-by-phase publication, uses the four-transition structure rNPV is actually built on, and does not impute unresolved trial outcomes. A uniform 0.85× drift factor is applied: industry likelihood of approval fell from 7.9% (2011–20) to 6.7% (2014–23) per Citeline.

| Area | Ph1 → approval | Ph2 | Ph3 | Filed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rare disease | 14.4% | 21.4% | 48.1% | 79.6% |
| Metabolic | 13.2% | 21.3% | 47.3% | 74.4% |
| Immuno-oncology | 10.5% | 16.5% | 41.0% | 83.6% |
| Immunology | 9.1% | 16.4% | 52.2% | 80.0% |
| Vaccines | 8.2% | 15.6% | 49.4% | 85.0% |
| **All indications** | **6.7%** | 12.9% | 44.5% | 77.0% |
| Psychiatry | 6.2% | 11.7% | 43.6% | 77.5% |
| **Neurology** | **5.0%** | 10.5% | 39.1% | 73.7% |
| Oncology | 4.5% | 9.2% | 37.3% | 78.2% |
| Cardiovascular | 4.1% | 8.1% | 38.7% | 70.1% |

**On the CNS number, since it is my focus.** Neurology's 5.9% raw LOA (5.0% drift-adjusted) is confirmed by three independent datasets — BIO 2011–2020 (n=1,411), BIO 2006–2015, and Tufts CSDD (6.2% on a 1995–2013 cohort). But the *commonly cited* framing of "6% CNS versus 10–12% industry" is wrong, because 10–12% comes from Hay 2014 and Kola & Landis, whose data ends in 2011. Against a correct contemporaneous benchmark of 7.9%, neurology's penalty is **0.75×, not 0.5×**. The gap narrowed — and not because CNS improved. The rest of the industry got worse.

The more interesting structural point: CNS has no single bottleneck. It underperforms by 3–5 percentage points at *every* transition, and the shortfall compounds. Uniquely, that includes filing→approval (86.7% vs 90.6% industry, second-worst of any area after cardiovascular). That last gate is regulatory, not biological — it points at endpoint and clinical-meaningfulness disputes with FDA, not at CNS penetration. Oncology has the opposite shape: it fails on data (Ph3→filing 47.7%) but clears regulatory review above average (92.0%). **Oncology fails on evidence; neurology fails on evidence and on whether the evidence counts.**

**Modifiers are combined, not multiplied.** Lead-indication status, biomarker preselection, genetic target validation and rare-disease designation are each reported marginally against the all-indication baseline, and they are heavily correlated with each other. Stacking them raw gives a 49× uplift and probabilities above 1.0. They are combined in log-odds space with a 0.55 damping exponent and a hard 3.5× cap:

| Modifiers on a Ph2 neurology asset | Modelled PoS | Naive stacking |
|---|---|---|
| none | 10.5% | 10.5% |
| + lead indication | 21.6% | 32.5% |
| + biomarker preselection | 30.2% | 68.3% |
| + genetic target validation | 36.7% *(capped)* | 177.5% |
| + rare disease | 36.7% *(capped)* | 390.5% |

**Clinical risk sits in the PoS term, not the discount rate.** 10% WACC — Baras, Baras & Schulman (*Nat Rev Drug Discov* 2012, median 10%), DiMasi 2016 (10.5% real), Alacrita (10–13%), WIPO. Applying a venture-style 20–40% rate *and* probability weights double-counts the same risk. It is the single most common error in published biotech valuation and it is worth being able to explain in an interview.

**What the rNPV column means.** It values one asset, lead indication, one exclusivity window, at commercial assumptions from Tendler et al. (*Ther Innov Regul Sci* 2026, n=391 launches 2000–2023: median peak $462M, 6.4 years to peak, ~7-year plateau). It is not a price target. So the output is expressed as **coverage** — what share of the takeout price the lead asset alone explains:

| Coverage | Reading |
|---|---|
| Kura Oncology **100%** | An approved drug at an enterprise value near cash. You are buying the product and nothing else. |
| Travere 72%, Structure 59%, Madrigal 50% | Product-led. The lead asset carries most of the price. |
| Summit 36%, Cytokinetics 34%, Insmed 30% | Roughly a third product, two-thirds franchise expansion and pipeline. |
| Kymera **9%**, Revolution **6%**, Janux **5%** | You are buying a platform. Daraxonrasib in pancreatic cancer explains 6% of Revolution's takeout price — the other 94% is the rest of the RAS franchise. |

That is a statement about *what you are paying for*, not a claim the market is mispriced. Low coverage is not a sell signal; it tells you where the diligence has to go.

**The empirical model, and why it is shipped unrun.** `pos_train.py` reconstructs development paths from AACT or ClinicalTrials.gov, labels each trial by whether its program later registered a higher-phase trial, and fits a calibrated gradient-boosted classifier cross-validated with folds grouped by sponsor. It is validated on synthetic data with a planted signal — CV AUC 0.72, exact recovery of the planted therapeutic-area ranking, calibration tight across all ten deciles.

It is not run here because this environment has no network egress. More importantly, its output **blends with the priors rather than replacing them**, because the registry label is biased upward three ways: right-censoring (a 2024 Phase 2 has had no time to spawn a Phase 3), unregistered or renamed follow-on programs that look like failures, and silent discontinuation that looks identical to slowness. The script prints its own observed transition rates against the BIO priors and flags a likely censoring leak if Phase 2→3 lands more than 40% above BIO's 28.9%. **The cross-area ranking it produces is trustworthy; the absolute levels are not.** Building a model that says so, rather than one that reports a headline number, is the point.

**Weakest input, stated plainly: the peak-sales estimates are mine.** Commercial-stage peaks are scaled off reported revenue; clinical-stage default to area medians. A real desk takes these from FactSet or Evaluate consensus. They drive the rNPV linearly, so they drive the coverage column linearly, and they are the first thing to replace.
